Best by, use by, sell by: what food date labels really mean

By Dhiraj Pandey, maker of Tum Tum · November 16, 2026 · 10 min read

In the United States, most dates on food packages are about quality, not safety. Except for infant formula, federal law does not require date labels on food, though some states require dates on certain foods, such as milk. A "best by" date tells you when the food should taste its best, and food stored properly can often be fine after it, as long as it shows no signs of spoiling.

That does not mean every date can be ignored. Here is what each phrase means, where the line is, and how to decide what to keep.

Are food expiration dates required by law?

For most foods, no. The FDA says that, except for infant formula, manufacturers are not required by federal law or regulation to put quality-based date labels on packaged food. Companies add dates by choice and for their own reasons, which is why the wording changes from one package to the next.

The FDA lists some of the phrases you may see: "Sell By," "Best By," "Expires on" and others. In late 2024 the FDA and USDA asked the public for input on date labeling, noting that different phrases may confuse shoppers and lead people to throw out food that is still safe and wholesome.

What each date label means

There is no single legal definition for most of these phrases. The meanings below come from the FDA and from university extension services.

Best if used by / Best by / Best before

This is a quality date. It tells you when the food is expected to be at its best flavor and texture. The FDA supports making "Best if Used By" the standard phrase for quality dates, and the FDA and USDA both recommend it to the food industry. The goal is to make clear that the date is about quality, not safety.

The FDA's advice for food past this date is to look at it before deciding. If it has changed noticeably in color, consistency or texture, you may want to skip it.

Sell by

This date is mainly for the store. Virginia Cooperative Extension describes it as a way to let the store know when it should sell the product. You often see it on dairy and bakery items. It is not a deadline for eating the food at home. UConn Extension says items are generally safe to eat after the sell-by date, though they may start to lose flavor or look less appealing.

Use by

USDA's Food Safety and Inspection Service calls it the last date recommended for using the product at peak quality, and says it is not a safety date except on infant formula. Since it is the maker's last recommended date, the simple habit is to use the food by then, or freeze it before then if it freezes well.

Freeze by

Virginia Cooperative Extension explains that "freeze by" (sometimes "use or freeze by") tells you when to freeze a perishable food, like meat, to keep its quality until you eat it. If you will not cook it in time, put it in the freezer before that date.

Infant formula is the exception

Infant formula is the one product where a date is required by federal rules. It must carry a "Use By" date. Up to that date, the manufacturer has confirmed that the formula contains at least the minimum amount of each nutrient. Virginia Cooperative Extension's advice is simple: never use formula after its "use by" date.

This is a firm rule, not a quality suggestion. Babies rely on formula for their nutrition, so the date matters in a way it does not for a box of crackers.

Is food safe after the best by date?

Often it is, if it has been stored the right way. Virginia Cooperative Extension says foods can still be good quality and safe to eat after these dates. But storage matters more than the printed date. Food kept too warm, or left out too long, can spoil before its date.

So the real question is not just "what does the label say?" but also "how was this kept?" A carton of milk left in a hot car is not protected by a date that is still a week away.

Signs that food has gone bad

Your eyes and nose are good at judging quality on food that was stored properly. They cannot prove food is safe, and they never stretch the 3 to 4 day limit on leftovers. Throw food out if you see:

  • Mold, whether white, green or black
  • An off or sour smell
  • A change in texture, like slime or stickiness
  • A change in color

A smell test is not a full safety check, so it does not replace good storage. If you are unsure about something, it is safer to throw it out.

Dates on common foods, one by one

The same phrase can mean different things on different foods, and some packages carry codes instead of words. Here is how to read the ones you see most.

Eggs

Many egg cartons carry two things: a printed date and a small three-digit number. University of Nebraska-Lincoln Extension explains that the number is the pack date, counted as a day of the year. 001 is January 1 and 365 is December 31, so 032 means the eggs were packed on February 1.

The printed "sell by" or "EXP" date is not required by federal law, and UNL Extension notes that some state egg laws do not allow a "sell by" date at all. Buy eggs before the date on the carton. At home, the FDA says to refrigerate them at 40°F or below, keep them in their original carton on a shelf rather than the door, and use them within 3 weeks for best quality. UNL Extension adds that the sell-by date will often pass while you still have eggs left, and that is normal.

Milk

Milk is the classic example of a state rule. There is no federal date requirement, but some states require a date on milk. Keep it cold, keep it off the door and check it before you pour. If it smells sour or looks lumpy, toss it. We did not find one clear federal number for how long milk lasts after its date, so we will not give one here.

Canned goods

Many cans have no easy date at all, just a string of letters and numbers. Clemson Cooperative Extension says these "closed" or coded dates are packing numbers the maker uses to rotate stock. North Dakota State University Extension adds that each maker can have its own system, so you may need to contact the company to learn what a code means.

For quality, Clemson Extension says unopened high-acid canned foods, like tomatoes, juices and pickles, keep best for 12 to 18 months. Low-acid canned foods, like meat, most vegetables and beans, keep best for 2 to 5 years. University of Illinois Extension notes that canned foods stay safe for many years after the date on the can, though taste and texture may change.

The can itself matters more than the code. The FDA says to throw out cans that are swollen, leaking, punctured or cracked, have deep rust, or are dented badly enough that they won't stack or open normally. Illinois Extension explains that a swollen can may be a sign of Clostridium botulinum, the germ that causes botulism. Illinois Extension says not to use food from swollen cans.

Bread

Bread usually carries a "best by" date, which is about quality. NDSU Extension explains that freezing bread delays staling and that bread stays at high quality for about three months in the freezer. Seal it in an airtight bag and write the date you froze it on the bag. If bread grows mold, NDSU Extension says to throw it away.

Infant formula and baby food

As covered above, infant formula must be used by its "use by" date. The FDA adds that some baby foods must be used by the date on the package too. Check baby food jars and pouches, and when in doubt, do not use it past the date.

Once you open the package

A printed date describes the food in its sealed package. UConn Extension points out that once a package is opened, many of these dates no longer apply, because the contents are now perishable. A jar of salsa with a "best by" date next year still needs the fridge once it is open.

The FDA reminds shoppers to check the storage directions on labels, since many foods besides meat and dairy need refrigeration. Illinois Extension notes that some shelf-stable foods must be refrigerated once opened, so read the package. For leftover canned food, Clemson Extension says to move it into a covered glass or plastic container in the fridge.

Dates and your freezer

Freezing changes how much a date matters. The FDA says food kept at 0°F (-18°C) stays safe indefinitely. Freezing does not kill most bacteria, but it does stop them from growing. What changes over time is quality: texture, flavor and color slowly decline.

That is why the "freeze by" habit works. Freeze meat or bread before its date, write the date you froze it on the package, and eat older packages first. Freezer burn, the dry gray-brown spots on poorly wrapped food, is a quality problem, not a safety problem, according to the FDA.

Why this matters

Throwing out good food because of a confusing label is common. The FDA has estimated that confusion over date labels accounts for about 20 percent of consumer food waste, as stated on its food waste page. Knowing which dates are about quality and which you should follow closely can keep perfectly good food out of the trash.

A few habits help:

  • Put food that is close to its date in a spot you will see first. See how to organize your fridge for an "eat first" bin idea.
  • Freeze meat and bread before their dates if you won't use them in time.
  • Buy smaller amounts of foods you often throw out.
  • Keep a short list of what you have so dates don't sneak up on you. Our guide to keeping a kitchen inventory shows a few easy ways. Tum Tum, our iPhone app, does this from a receipt photo and shows what to eat first, but a paper list works too.

Quick answers

Is it safe to eat food after the best by date?

Often, yes, if it was stored properly and shows no signs of spoiling. The FDA suggests checking it first and skipping it if its color, consistency or texture has changed noticeably.

Is "use by" a safety date?

Not on most foods. USDA FSIS calls it the last date recommended for peak quality. Infant formula is the exception: never use it after its "use by" date.

What does the number on my egg carton mean?

The three-digit number is usually the pack date, counted as a day of the year from 001 (January 1) to 365 (December 31).

How long do canned goods last?

For best quality, Clemson Extension says 12 to 18 months for high-acid foods like tomatoes and 2 to 5 years for low-acid foods like vegetables and beans. Store cans somewhere cool and dry; Clemson suggests between 50 and 70°F, away from the stove and direct sun.

Can I eat food from a dented can?

It depends on the damage. The FDA says to throw out cans that are swollen, leaking or dented badly enough that they won't stack or open normally.

Does freezing stop the clock?

For safety, yes: the FDA says food kept at 0°F stays safe indefinitely. For quality, no. It slowly declines, so date what you freeze and eat it in order.

Who can I ask about a date I don't understand?

The FDA suggests contacting the company that made the food. Its contact details are often on the package. For general storage times, the FDA points to the FoodKeeper guide from USDA.
