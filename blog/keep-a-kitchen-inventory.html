How to keep a kitchen inventory (without a spreadsheet)

By Dhiraj Pandey, maker of Tum Tum · November 16, 2026 · 10 min read

A kitchen inventory is just a list of the food you have and where it is. You do not need a spreadsheet. A paper list on the fridge, a whiteboard or a note on your phone works fine, as long as you keep it short and check it once a week.

You don't need to track much. Here is why it is worth doing, a few simple ways to do it, and how to keep it going without it turning into a chore.

Why keep a kitchen inventory?

Most of us have bought a second jar of something we already had, or found a bag of spinach that turned to liquid in the back of the drawer. A list fixes both problems.

  • Less double buying. The FDA suggests checking your fridge before you shop so you know what you already have. A list lets you do that from the store aisle.
  • Less wasted food. The FDA also suggests checking your fridge often to keep track of what you have and what needs to be used. Food you can see on a list is food you remember to eat.
  • Easier dinners. When you know you have chicken thighs, half a bag of rice and a can of tomatoes, "what's for dinner?" is half answered.
  • Better shopping lists. Your inventory tells you what is running low, so the shopping list nearly writes itself.
  • Money kept. The EPA estimates that the average family of four spends almost $3,000 a year on food that does not get eaten. Knowing what you have is one of the simplest ways to bring that down.

What to track (and what to skip)

The biggest reason people give up on a kitchen inventory is that they try to track too much. You are not running a warehouse.

For most homes, two things are enough:

  1. The name of the food. "Greek yogurt," not "Greek yogurt, 32 oz, plain, 2% fat."
  2. Where it is. Fridge, freezer, pantry or counter.

A rough amount can help for things you run through fast, like "eggs: a few left." A date helps for leftovers and freezer meat. Utah State University Extension suggests keeping a marker nearby so you can write the date on items that don't have one, like bulk food and meat you freeze.

What to skip: exact weights, every spice jar, and things you always have, like salt. If keeping the list takes more than a few minutes a week, trim it.

A sample inventory

Here is what a real list might look like for a small household on a Sunday afternoon. Notice how short it is. Dates appear only where they help.

  • Fridge: eggs (half a carton), milk, Greek yogurt, cheddar, leftover chili (made Fri), raw chicken thighs (cook by Mon), spinach (half bag), carrots, salsa (opened)
  • Freezer: ground beef (frozen Oct 30), 2 bags frozen peas, half a loaf of bread (frozen Nov 9), chili portion (frozen Nov 2)
  • Pantry: rice, pasta, 3 cans black beans, 2 cans tomatoes, peanut butter, oats, onions, potatoes
  • Counter: bananas, 3 tomatoes, 2 unripe avocados

From this one list, the week's plan almost writes itself. The chicken thighs and spinach need eating first. The leftover chili should be eaten by Monday, since cooked leftovers keep 3 to 4 days in the fridge. Tomatoes, beans and rice can carry a second dinner. The shopping list is short, because you can see you already have pasta, eggs and peas.

Tips for each place

Fridge

The fridge is where food turns over fastest, so it deserves the most attention. Put raw meat and leftovers on the list with a date. Leftovers keep 3 to 4 days, so a date tells you at a glance what to eat tonight. For more, see how long leftovers last.

Freezer

The freezer is where food gets forgotten longest. The EPA suggests visiting your freezer often and labeling what goes in with its contents and the date. The FDA says food kept at 0°F (-18°C) stays safe indefinitely, but its texture, flavor and color slowly decline. So a freezer list is less about safety and more about eating things while they still taste good.

As a guide, Virginia Cooperative Extension suggests 2 to 3 months in the freezer for cooked meat and meat dishes, and 3 to 5 months for store-bought bread. Write the freeze date on your list next to each item, and eat the oldest first.

Pantry

Pantry food lasts long, which is exactly why it piles up. Virginia Cooperative Extension gives these room-temperature times for best quality:

Pantry foodKeeps for best quality
All-purpose white flour6 to 12 months
Whole wheat flour3 to 6 months
Brown rice1 year
Dry pasta2 years
Ready-to-eat cereal6 to 12 months
Peanut butter6 to 24 months
Canned fruit and fruit juice12 to 18 months
Canned vegetables2 to 5 years

Virginia Extension says to keep dry goods below 85°F, ideally between 50 and 70°F. University of Illinois Extension adds a few pantry rules: keep the area dry, keep food off the floor, store food away from cleaning supplies, and put opened food in a bag or a container with a lid to keep pests out. Some shelf-stable foods need the fridge once opened, so read the package. For what dates on these packages mean, see best by vs use by.

Counter

The counter is for foods that do better outside the fridge, like bananas, whole tomatoes and unripe avocados. These go fast and are easy to see, so a one-word entry is plenty. For which foods belong where, see where to store fruits and vegetables.

Simple ways to keep an inventory

Pick the one that fits how your household already works. The best method is the one you will actually keep up.

A paper list on the fridge

Tape a sheet of paper to the fridge door, split into four boxes: Fridge, Freezer, Pantry, Counter. Write things down when you unpack groceries. Cross them off when they are gone. It is free, everyone in the house can see it, and nobody needs a password.

The weak spot: you can't see it from the store. Snap a photo of it before you leave.

A whiteboard

Same idea, but easier to erase. A small magnetic whiteboard on the freezer door works well for the freezer, since that is where food gets forgotten longest. Some people keep a "use this week" column at the top.

A shared note on your phone

A note in your phone's notes app, shared with the people you live with, goes with you to the store. Use one heading per place and keep each item to one line. It works best when one person does the updating after each shop.

The receipt method

Keep your grocery receipt on the fridge under a magnet. That receipt is your list of what came in this week. As you use things up, cross them off. At the end of the week, anything not crossed off is what you should eat next. It is not perfect, since receipt names can be hard to read, but it costs nothing and takes no writing.

An app

An app makes sense if writing things down is the part you keep skipping. The good ones save typing and remind you before food goes bad. The trade-off is that you are relying on a phone instead of a sheet of paper everyone can see.

The weekly 5-minute check

Whatever method you choose, a short weekly check keeps it honest. The day before you shop is a good time.

  1. Open the fridge. Toss anything spoiled. Cross it off.
  2. Look for food that needs eating soon. Move it to the front or into an "eat first" spot. The FDA suggests keeping a designated space in your fridge for food that will go bad within a few days.
  3. Glance at the freezer and pantry. Update anything that is gone or running low.
  4. Plan two or three meals around the food that needs eating first.
  5. Write your shopping list from what is missing. The FDA recommends writing a list before you go to the store.

That is it. Five minutes, once a week.

The EPA suggests a similar habit: each week, make a list of what needs to be used up and plan your next meals around it. It also suggests noting on your shopping list how many meals you will make with each item, so you don't overbuy.

Common mistakes

  • Tracking everything. A list with 80 lines is a list nobody updates. Start with fridge and freezer, then add the pantry if you want.
  • Updating only after big shops. The list drifts when small trips and takeout leftovers never make it on. A quick line when you unpack anything keeps it honest.
  • No dates on freezer food. A bag of frozen meat with no date is a guess. Write the date on the package and on the list.
  • Never crossing things off. If used-up food stays on the list, you stop trusting it. Cross off as you go, or clean it up during the weekly check.
  • One person carrying it alone. If others in the house eat the last yogurt, they should cross it off. Keep the list where everyone can reach it.
  • Buying the deal you won't finish. The EPA notes that buying in large amounts, like buy-one-get-one deals, only saves money if you use all the food before it spoils. Check the list before you grab the second one.

First in, first out

An inventory works best with one unpacking habit. When new groceries come home, put them behind the older ones. University of Illinois Extension recommends rotating pantry food this way, called "first in, first out," to keep quality up and reduce food waste. Utah State University Extension gives the same advice for food storage and suggests dating and rotating freezer meat too.

Quick answers

How often should I update my kitchen inventory?

Add things when you unpack groceries and cross them off when they run out. Then do one short check a week, ideally the day before you shop.

Do I need to track amounts?

Not usually. A name and a place are enough for most foods. Add a rough amount only for things you run through fast, like eggs or milk.

Should I write dates on everything?

No. Dates help most on leftovers, raw meat and freezer food. Utah State University Extension suggests keeping a marker handy for items that come without a date, like bulk food.

What is the easiest place to start?

The freezer. Food stays there longest, so it is where a list saves the most forgotten food. A whiteboard on the freezer door is a good first step.

How do I keep a pantry inventory without it taking forever?

List categories, not every can. "Canned beans: 3" is enough. Put new items behind old ones so you use the older ones first.

Is a kitchen inventory worth it for one person?

Yes. When you live alone, half-used packages and single portions of leftovers are easy to lose track of. Even a five-line list helps.
