How to keep a kitchen inventory (without a spreadsheet)

By Dhiraj Pandey, maker of Tum Tum · November 16, 2026 · 5 min read

A kitchen inventory is just a list of the food you have and where it is. You do not need a spreadsheet. A paper list on the fridge, a whiteboard or a note on your phone works fine, as long as you keep it short and check it once a week.

You don't need to track much. Here is why it is worth doing, a few simple ways to do it, and how to keep it going without it turning into a chore.

Why keep a kitchen inventory?

Most of us have bought a second jar of something we already had, or found a bag of spinach that turned to liquid in the back of the drawer. A list fixes both problems.

  • Less double buying. The FDA suggests checking your fridge before you shop so you know what you already have. A list lets you do that from the store aisle.
  • Less wasted food. The FDA also suggests checking your fridge often to keep track of what you have and what needs to be used. Food you can see on a list is food you remember to eat.
  • Easier dinners. When you know you have chicken thighs, half a bag of rice and a can of tomatoes, "what's for dinner?" is half answered.
  • Better shopping lists. Your inventory tells you what is running low, so the shopping list nearly writes itself.

What to track (and what to skip)

The biggest reason people give up on a kitchen inventory is that they try to track too much. You are not running a warehouse.

For most homes, two things are enough:

  1. The name of the food. "Greek yogurt," not "Greek yogurt, 32 oz, plain, 2% fat."
  2. Where it is. Fridge, freezer, pantry or counter.

A rough amount can help for things you run through fast, like "eggs: a few left." A date helps for leftovers and freezer meat. Utah State University Extension suggests keeping a marker nearby so you can write the date on items that don't have one, like bulk food and meat you freeze.

What to skip: exact weights, every spice jar, and things you always have, like salt. If keeping the list takes more than a few minutes a week, trim it.

Simple ways to keep an inventory

Pick the one that fits how your household already works. The best method is the one you will actually keep up.

A paper list on the fridge

Tape a sheet of paper to the fridge door, split into four boxes: Fridge, Freezer, Pantry, Counter. Write things down when you unpack groceries. Cross them off when they are gone. It is free, everyone in the house can see it, and nobody needs a password.

The weak spot: you can't see it from the store. Snap a photo of it before you leave.

A whiteboard

Same idea, but easier to erase. A small magnetic whiteboard on the freezer door works well for the freezer, since that is where food gets forgotten longest. Some people keep a "use this week" column at the top.

A shared note on your phone

A note in your phone's notes app, shared with the people you live with, goes with you to the store. Use one heading per place and keep each item to one line. It works best when one person does the updating after each shop.

The receipt method

Keep your grocery receipt on the fridge under a magnet. That receipt is your list of what came in this week. As you use things up, cross them off. At the end of the week, anything not crossed off is what you should eat next. It is not perfect, since receipt names can be hard to read, but it costs nothing and takes no writing.

An app

An app makes sense if writing things down is the part you keep skipping. The good ones save typing and remind you before food goes bad. The trade-off is that you are relying on a phone instead of a sheet of paper everyone can see.

The weekly 5-minute check

Whatever method you choose, a short weekly check keeps it honest. The day before you shop is a good time.

  1. Open the fridge. Toss anything spoiled. Cross it off.
  2. Look for food that needs eating soon. Move it to the front or into an "eat first" spot. The FDA suggests keeping a designated space in your fridge for food that will go bad within a few days.
  3. Glance at the freezer and pantry. Update anything that is gone or running low.
  4. Plan two or three meals around the food that needs eating first.
  5. Write your shopping list from what is missing. The FDA recommends writing a list before you go to the store.

That is it. Five minutes, once a week.

First in, first out

An inventory works best with one unpacking habit. When new groceries come home, put them behind the older ones. University of Illinois Extension recommends rotating pantry food this way, called "first in, first out," to keep quality up and reduce food waste. Utah State University Extension gives the same advice for food storage and suggests dating and rotating freezer meat too.
