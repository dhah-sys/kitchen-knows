How to stop wasting food at home: 9 simple habits

By Dhiraj Pandey, maker of Tum Tum · November 16, 2026 · 10 min read

Most food waste at home comes from a few small slips: buying something you already had, forgetting what is in the back of the fridge, and cooking more than anyone will eat. The fix is not a big system. It is a handful of habits: look before you shop, buy for real meals, eat the oldest food first, and freeze things before they turn.

It adds up. The EPA says the average family of four spends almost $3,000 a year on food that does not get eaten. Here are the habits that make the biggest difference, in the order you would use them during a normal week.

1. Check what you already have before you shop

This is the easiest habit and the one people skip most. Before you write a list, open the fridge, the freezer and the pantry. The EPA suggests making a list each week of what needs to be used up, and planning the next few meals around it.

A quick way to do it:

  • Look for anything soft, wilting or open: half a bag of spinach, a cut onion, the last of the yogurt.
  • Write those down first. They are this week's starting point.
  • Check the freezer too. Many "we have nothing to eat" nights are solved by something already frozen.

2. Shop with a list built from meals, not wishes

A list is only useful if it matches how you actually eat. The EPA's advice is to plan the week's meals before you shop, buy only what those meals need, and count how often you will eat out or eat leftovers.

Write quantities on the list

"Salad greens" is easy to overbuy. "Salad greens, enough for two lunches" is not. The EPA uses that exact kind of note as an example, and it works because it ties each item to a meal.

Be careful with big deals

Buy-one-get-one and bulk packs only save money if you use all of it before it spoils. If you would not finish two bags of spinach in a week, the free one is not really free.

Buy the amount, not the package

Bulk bins let you scoop just what a recipe needs, such as half a cup of lentils instead of a two-pound bag. The EPA notes that this saves money and cuts waste. The EPA also points out that imperfect produce, with odd shapes or small marks, is just as safe and nutritious as the pretty kind, and it often costs less.

Keep a short list of meals your house already likes

Writing down the dinners your household already enjoys, with their ingredients, makes planning fast and means you buy food that gets eaten.

3. Put food away the right way

Good storage buys you days. A few basics:

  • Keep the fridge at 40°F (4°C) or below and the freezer at 0°F (-18°C). The FDA says an inexpensive appliance thermometer is the best way to know.
  • Do not pack the fridge so tightly that cold air cannot move around.
  • Put perishables away as soon as you get home. The FDA's two-hour rule says foods that need the fridge should not sit at room temperature for more than two hours, or one hour above 90°F.
  • Wait to wash berries, cherries and grapes until you are about to eat them. Extra moisture invites mold.
  • Keep eggs in their carton on a shelf, not in the door. The FDA says the door is warmer.
  • Eat ready-to-eat foods like lunch meat soon after opening. The FDA says these should be used as soon as possible.
  • Store rice, oats, flour and other grains in airtight containers with a label and date, as the EPA suggests. It keeps them fresh and easy to see.
  • In the pantry, keep food away from cleaning products and other household chemicals, per the FDA.

Fruits and vegetables have their own rules. Some lose flavor in the fridge and some go bad fast on the counter. Our guide on where to store fruits and vegetables covers that in detail, and how to organize your fridge covers shelves and drawers.

4. First in, first out

Grocery stores do this every day. When new milk arrives, the older carton moves to the front. You can do the same at home:

  1. When you unpack groceries, move what is already there to the front.
  2. Put the new items behind or below.
  3. Reach for the front first.

Most forgotten food is forgotten because it got pushed to the back.

5. Make an "eat this first" shelf

Pick one spot in the fridge, a shelf or a clear bin, for food that needs to go soon. Leftovers, the open jar of sauce, the last two peppers. When anyone opens the fridge looking for a snack or a lunch, they look there first.

It works because nobody has to dig around or remember what is getting old.

6. Plan for leftovers on purpose

Leftovers are only waste if nobody eats them. Two ways to make them count:

  • Plan a leftovers night. If you cook a big pot of chili on Sunday, put "chili" on Tuesday's plan too.
  • Turn odds and ends into a new meal. The EPA suggests using past-its-prime produce and leftovers in soups, casseroles, stir-fries, frittatas, sauces or smoothies.
  • Cook the right amount. The EPA's advice is to cook and serve portions that fit the number of people eating. A pot of rice for four does not need to be a pot of rice for eight.

Use the parts you usually throw away

Some of what goes in the trash is perfectly good food. The EPA gives a few easy examples:

  • Stale bread makes good French toast or croutons.
  • Beet greens can be sautéed as a side dish.
  • Vegetable scraps, like onion ends, carrot peels and celery tops, can go into soup stock. Keep a bag in the freezer and add to it until you have enough for a pot.

Store leftovers in clear containers with the date written on them, and keep an eye on the clock. Cooked leftovers keep about 3 to 4 days in the fridge. Our guide on how long leftovers last explains the safe way to cool, store and reheat them.

7. Freeze it before it turns

The freezer is the best tool against waste. Food kept at 0°F stays safe indefinitely, according to the FDA. Quality slowly drops over time, but you have far more room to work with than in the fridge.

Good candidates, from the EPA's list:

  • Bread you will not finish this week
  • Sliced fruit for smoothies or baking
  • Meat you will not cook before its time is up
  • Leftovers you know will not be eaten in time

Label everything with what it is and the date. A freezer full of mystery bags is just a slower way to waste food.

Freezing is not the only way to save extra food. When a garden or a good sale leaves you with too much of one thing, the EPA lists pickling, dehydrating, canning, and making jam or jelly as other options.

8. Understand date labels

Date labels cause a lot of good food to be thrown out. The USDA's Food Safety and Inspection Service (FSIS) says that, except for infant formula, product dates are not required by federal law and are not a sign of safety. They are about quality.

LabelWhat it means (per USDA FSIS)
Best if Used By / BeforeWhen the product will have its best flavor or quality. Not a purchase or safety date.
Sell-ByTells the store how long to display the product. Not a safety date.
Use-ByThe last date recommended for peak quality. Not a safety date, except on infant formula.
Freeze-ByWhen to freeze the product to keep peak quality. Not a purchase or safety date.

FSIS says food should still be safe after the date if it has been handled properly, until signs of spoilage show up, like an off smell, flavor or texture. That applies to date labels on packaged food; cooked leftovers follow their own 3 to 4 day rule. FSIS also notes that USDA estimates 30 percent of the food supply is lost or wasted at the retail and consumer levels, and confusion about dates is one cause.

One important limit: a date label is not a pass for food that was left out too long or stored warm. The FDA's advice still applies. If you are not sure, or the food looks questionable, throw it out. More on labels in our guide to best-by vs use-by dates.

9. Keep a simple record of what you have

All of these habits get easier when you can see what is in your kitchen without opening every door. A whiteboard on the fridge with two columns, "eat soon" and "have plenty," works. A simple weekly routine

  1. Check the fridge, freezer and pantry, and note what needs using.
  2. Plan meals around those items, plus one leftovers night.
  3. Shop from a list with quantities.
  4. Unpack with older food to the front.
  5. Midweek, freeze what you will not get to in time.

What one week can look like

Here is a real-life example. On Sunday morning, a quick look finds half a bag of spinach, four eggs, most of a rotisserie chicken bought Saturday, a few soft tomatoes, and a bag of frozen peas.

DayDinnerWhat it uses up
SundaySpinach and tomato frittataThe spinach, the eggs, the soft tomatoes
MondayChicken quesadillasHalf the chicken while it is still fresh
TuesdayChicken fried rice with peasThe rest of the chicken and the frozen peas
WednesdayA new meal from the shopping tripFresh food bought on Sunday
ThursdayLeftovers nightWhatever is left from Wednesday
FridaySoup or eat outThe vegetable odds and ends

The shopping list for that week is short, because three dinners were already in the fridge. The chicken is gone by Tuesday, well inside the 3 to 4 day window for cooked leftovers. Nothing new was bought for Sunday through Tuesday.

Common mistakes that waste food

  • Planning seven new recipes. Most weeks have a busy night or a surprise dinner out. Plan five meals and leave room for leftovers.
  • Putting new groceries in front. It is faster when you unpack, but it hides the older food.
  • Freezing on the last day. The freezer does not make old food new again. Freeze extras early, while they are still at their best, not on the day they are about to turn.
  • Throwing food out on the sell-by date. That date is for the store, not for you.
  • Trusting the sniff test for leftovers. The FDA notes that food can make you sick even when it looks and smells fine. Go by the date you wrote on the container.
  • Keeping a list nobody updates. A list is only useful if it matches what is really on the shelves.

When food still goes unused

Some waste will happen, even in a careful kitchen. The EPA suggests two better places for it than the trash. Safe, untouched food, like unopened cans you will not use, can be donated to a food bank. Food scraps can be composted instead of thrown away.

A checklist to keep on the fridge

  • Fridge at 40°F (4°C) or below, freezer at 0°F (-18°C), checked once a week
  • An "eat this first" shelf, emptied before the next shop
  • Leftovers in clear containers with the date
  • A freezer bag for vegetable scraps
  • A shopping list with quantities, written after checking the shelves
  • One leftovers night on the plan

Quick answers

How much money can cutting food waste save?

The EPA says a family of four could save up to $56 a week by throwing away less food.

Is "ugly" produce safe to eat?

Yes. The EPA says imperfect produce is just as safe and nutritious. The marks are only on the outside.

Does freezing food destroy its nutrients?

No. The FDA says freezing does not reduce nutrients. What changes over time is quality, like texture and flavor.

How often should I check my fridge temperature?

At least once a week, according to the FDA, using an appliance thermometer.

Should I keep a dented can?

The FDA says to throw away cans that are swollen, leaking, punctured, cracked or deeply rusted. If you just bought a leaking can, return it to the store.

Where should eggs go in the fridge?

In their carton, on a shelf inside the fridge. The FDA advises against the door because it is warmer there.

You do not need to do all of this at once. Start by checking what you have before you shop.
