Where to store fruits and vegetables: fridge, counter or pantry?

By Dhiraj Pandey, maker of Tum Tum · November 16, 2026 · 10 min read

Most berries, grapes, leafy greens, broccoli, carrots and other crisp vegetables belong in the fridge. Bananas, whole tomatoes, citrus, melons and unripe avocados or peaches do better on the counter. Potatoes, onions, garlic and sweet potatoes want a cool, dark, airy pantry spot, and potatoes should be kept away from onions and fruit.

Getting this right matters for two reasons. Food lasts longer, and it tastes better. The cold of a fridge can stop some fruit from ripening and give some vegetables an off flavor. Below is a full table, then the reasons behind it.

Quick guide: fridge, counter or pantry

This table follows the University of California, Davis guide Storing Fresh Fruits and Vegetables for Better Taste, with notes from Michigan State University Extension and the EPA.

UC Davis notes that cucumbers, eggplant and peppers can go in the fridge for 1 to 3 days if you use them soon after taking them out. Some sources, including the EPA, simply put peppers and cucumbers in the fridge. If you will use them within a few days, either way is fine.

What goes on the counter

Some produce is hurt by the cold. UC Davis explains that a typical fridge runs about 38 to 42°F, (food safety guidance says to keep it at 40°F or below), and that cold damages certain fruits and vegetables or stops them from ripening to a good flavor and texture.

Bananas

In the fridge, bananas get black skins and never reach full sweetness. Keep them on the counter.

Whole tomatoes

Tomatoes do not turn red in the fridge, and even red ones lose flavor there, according to UC Davis. North Dakota State University Extension says fresh tomatoes keep good quality for about five days at room temperature. Once you cut a tomato, put the rest in the fridge.

Melons

Watermelon loses flavor and its deep red color if kept in the fridge longer than about 3 days. Whole melons belong on the counter. Cut melon goes in the fridge, like all cut fruit.

How to keep counter produce happy

  • Keep it out of direct sunlight so it does not get too warm.
  • Use an open or vented bowl, or a bag with holes.
  • Do not seal produce in a closed plastic bag on the counter. UC Davis says this slows ripening and can lead to off odors and decay.

Ripen on the counter, then move to the fridge

Avocados, peaches, nectarines, plums, pears and kiwifruit are often sold hard. Leave them on the counter until they are ripe, then move them to the fridge. UC Davis says ripe fruit from this group can stay in the fridge for 1 to 3 days without losing taste.

Bartlett pears are a good example. UC Davis says they turn yellow and get softer and sweeter on the counter. So let them ripen first, then chill them.

To speed things up, put the fruit in a bowl or paper bag with one ripe apple for every 5 to 7 pieces. Apples give off ethylene, a natural gas that speeds ripening. UC Davis notes that Fuji and Granny Smith apples give off little ethylene, so they will not help much.

What goes in the fridge

Berries, grapes, cherries, greens and most crisp vegetables last much longer cold. A few tips make a real difference:

  • Use the drawers. UC Davis recommends perforated plastic bags in the produce drawers. If your bags have no holes, you can poke about 20 small holes in a medium bag.
  • Fruit in one drawer, vegetables in the other. This keeps the ethylene from fruit away from vegetables.
  • Know your humidity settings. The EPA suggests the high-humidity drawer for things that wilt, like leafy greens, carrots and broccoli, and the low-humidity drawer for most fruits and for vegetables that tend to rot, like mushrooms.
  • Wait to wash. The EPA says to wash berries, cherries and grapes only when you are ready to eat them, to help prevent mold.
  • Use it within a few days. Even in the fridge, longer storage means less freshness and flavor.

Carrots

NDSU Extension says carrots keep best cool and moist, with the leafy tops trimmed to about half an inch from the crown. Stored that way, whole carrots can last for months. Prewashed or cut carrots, like baby carrots, always go in the fridge.

What goes in a cool, dark pantry

Potatoes, sweet potatoes, onions and garlic do not belong in the fridge or in a sunny spot. UC Davis says to keep them in a well-ventilated area in the pantry. The EPA adds winter squash to that list of foods that like a cool, dry, dark, airy place.

Potatoes

Michigan State University Extension says potatoes keep best in a dark, cool place, ideally 45 to 55°F. A fridge is too cold. It can turn some of the starch to sugar, which gives an unpleasant flavor. Store potatoes in an open bin or a bag with holes so air can move.

Keep them in the dark, because light can turn them green. MSU says small green spots can be trimmed off, but if the green covers a large area, throw the potato out. A firm potato with small sprouts is fine once the sprouts are removed. A soft, shriveled one is not.

Sweet potatoes

UC Davis says refrigerated sweet potatoes can develop off flavors and a hard core when cooked. Keep them in the pantry.

Winter squash

Butternut, Hubbard and other winter squash are some of the longest-keeping vegetables you can buy. NDSU Extension says squash that has been cured (a step growers do for about two weeks in a warm, airy place) can last six months or longer in a cool, dry spot. Once you cut one, wrap the rest and refrigerate it like any cut vegetable.

Onions and garlic

Whole dry onions and garlic want a cool, dry spot with good airflow. A mesh bag or open basket works well. Once you cut an onion, wrap it and keep it in the fridge.

What to keep apart

Some fruits give off ethylene as they ripen. The EPA names bananas, apples, pears, stone fruits and avocados. Ethylene speeds ripening in anything nearby, which is helpful in a paper bag of hard peaches and harmful in your vegetable drawer.

The UC Master Gardener Program lists what ethylene can do to sensitive produce:

  • Green vegetables turn yellow
  • Lettuce gets brown, rusty spots
  • Asparagus gets tough
  • Carrots turn bitter
  • Potatoes sprout

So keep bananas, apples and avocados away from greens, lettuce, carrots and broccoli, and do not store ripe fruit in the vegetable drawer.

Keep onions and potatoes apart

They look like natural neighbors, but Michigan State University Extension says not to store potatoes with onions, apples or other fruit, because they give off ethylene gas that can make potatoes sprout. Use two separate baskets, ideally on different shelves.

Cut, peeled and bagged produce

Everything above is about whole produce. Once you cut it, the rules change. UC Davis says all cut or prepared fruits and vegetables should go in the fridge promptly, and leftovers should be refrigerated within two hours of serving. That includes a cut tomato, half a melon, sliced peppers and a peeled orange.

The FDA adds two tips for shopping:

  • Buy pre-cut or bagged produce, like half a watermelon or a bag of salad greens, only if the store keeps it cold, in a fridge case or on ice.
  • Refrigerate all pre-cut and packaged produce as soon as you get home.

How to wash produce

Wash produce right before you eat or cook it, not before you store it. NDSU Extension says water speeds spoilage, so brush off extra soil before storing and dry anything you rinse. When it is time to eat, the FDA and UC Davis agree on these steps:

  1. Wash your hands with soap and warm water for at least 20 seconds.
  2. Cut away bruised or damaged spots. Throw out produce that looks rotten.
  3. Rinse under running water. UC Davis says soaking is not recommended because it gives germs a better chance to spread.
  4. Scrub firm produce, like melons, cucumbers and apples, with a clean produce brush.
  5. Dry with a clean cloth or paper towel.

Skip the soap. The FDA does not recommend washing produce with soap, detergent or commercial produce wash. Rinse even things you will peel, like oranges or melons, so dirt on the skin does not end up on the knife and the fruit inside.

Bagged greens marked pre-washed or ready-to-eat can be eaten without washing again, per the FDA. UC Davis notes that salad mixes sold loose, without that label, should be washed.

Keep produce away from raw meat

Fruits and vegetables you eat raw never get cooked, so nothing kills germs that land on them. The FDA and UC Davis both say to keep produce apart from raw meat, poultry and seafood, starting in the shopping cart and continuing at home. In the fridge, keep produce in its own drawers, away from meat juices. In the kitchen, the FDA suggests one cutting board for produce and another for raw meat, or washing the board, knives and counter with hot soapy water between the two.

Common mistakes

  • Putting everything in the fridge. Tomatoes, bananas and potatoes all do worse there.
  • Washing before storing. Extra moisture speeds spoilage. Wash just before eating, and dry anything you rinse.
  • One big fruit bowl for everything. Bananas next to everything else will ripen the whole bowl faster.
  • Forgetting the pantry basket. Potatoes and onions last a long time, which also makes them easy to forget until they sprout.

Quick answers

Should apples go in the fridge?

It depends on how soon you will eat them. UC Davis puts apples you will eat within a week on the counter and apples you will keep longer in the fridge.

Why do my fridge potatoes taste sweet?

Cold turns some of their starch into sugar. NDSU Extension notes this can change the flavor and color of cooked dishes. A cool, dark pantry spot avoids it.

Can I store a cut tomato on the counter?

No. NDSU Extension says to refrigerate cut tomatoes. Whole ones stay on the counter.

Do I need a special produce wash?

No. The FDA says running water is the way to go and does not recommend soap, detergent or commercial produce washes.

Do I have to wash bagged salad?

Not if the bag says pre-washed or ready-to-eat, according to the FDA. If you do rinse it, use a clean bowl and clean hands.

Are raw sprouts safe?

The FDA says sprouts can carry bacteria such as Salmonella, Listeria and E. coli, even when grown at home. It advises children, older adults, pregnant women and people with weakened immune systems to avoid raw or lightly cooked sprouts.

How long does produce last in the fridge?

Not as long as most people hope. UC Davis says to use refrigerated fruits and vegetables within a few days, because flavor and freshness fade with time.
