How to organize your fridge so food doesn't go bad

By Dhiraj Pandey, maker of Tum Tum · November 16, 2026 · 10 min read

A well organized fridge does two jobs. It keeps food cold enough to stay safe, and it keeps food where you can see it, so you eat it before it spoils. The short version: keep the fridge at 40°F (4°C) or below, put ready-to-eat food on the top shelves, keep raw meat wrapped on the bottom shelf, keep milk and eggs off the door, and give food that needs eating soon its own spot.

Below is the longer version, shelf by shelf, with the reasons behind each rule.

Start with the temperature

The FDA says to keep your refrigerator at or below 40°F (4°C) and your freezer at 0°F (-18°C). Start there: a fridge thermometer is the easiest way to check.

The dial inside your fridge does not tell you the temperature. It is just a setting. The FDA recommends a cheap appliance thermometer: put one in the fridge and one in the freezer, near the front where you can read it easily, and check them at least once a week.

Ohio State University Extension suggests setting the fridge air between 37 and 39°F, so food stays just under 40°F even when the door is opened a lot. If your thermometer reads above 40°F, turn the setting colder and check again the next day.

What goes on each shelf

A simple way to remember it, from Ohio State University Extension: store food top to bottom by how much cooking it needs. Food that gets no more cooking goes high. Food that must be cooked the most goes low. That way, if anything drips, it drips onto food that will be cooked anyway, not onto your salad.

Top shelves: ready-to-eat food

Leftovers, cooked dishes, deli items, hummus, washed fruit and anything you eat without cooking. Put leftovers in clear, covered containers so you can see what is inside. North Carolina Cooperative Extension suggests labeling containers with the date so you know when to toss them. For how long specific leftovers keep, see how long leftovers last.

Milk and eggs: on a shelf, not the door

The key point for milk and eggs is that they live on a shelf inside the fridge, not on the door. Any inside shelf works; just keep them above or well away from raw meat, which belongs at the bottom in a container. Keep eggs in their original carton. The FDA says to store eggs in the carton on a shelf rather than on the door, where it is warmer, and to use them within 3 weeks for best quality. Milk belongs here too. North Carolina Cooperative Extension notes that the door's temperature changes more than the inside of the fridge, so milk should not live there.

Bottom shelf: raw meat, poultry and seafood

Raw meat, chicken and fish go on the lowest shelf, wrapped and set in a container or on a tray. Ohio State University Extension recommends this so juices cannot leak onto other food. The EPA adds a second reason: the lower shelves are the coldest part of the fridge. Store packages can leak, so the container is worth it even when the wrap looks sealed. The FDA's broader rule is the same idea: keep raw foods separate from ready-to-eat foods, and store refrigerated food in covered containers or sealed bags.

Crisper drawers: set the humidity

Many crisper drawers have a small slider. North Carolina Cooperative Extension explains what the settings are for:

  • High humidity (vent closed): produce that wilts easily, like leafy greens and fresh herbs.
  • Low humidity (vent open): produce that ripens quickly, like apples, pears and avocados.

The EPA also suggests waiting to wash berries, cherries and grapes until you are ready to eat them, since washing early invites mold. If you have two drawers, use one for each. For which fruits and vegetables belong in the fridge at all, see where to store fruits and vegetables.

The door: condiments only

The door is the warmest part of the fridge and swings in and out of room air all day. North Carolina Cooperative Extension says condiments are fine there because they keep longer. Ketchup, mustard, jam, pickles, soy sauce and salad dressing are good door foods. Milk and eggs are not.

How long common foods keep in the fridge

Where food sits is half the job. The other half is knowing how long it can stay. These times come from University of Illinois Extension, for a fridge kept between 35 and 40°F. They are limits, not goals: Illinois Extension advises using refrigerated food quickly and not depending on the maximum time.

FoodFridge time
Raw ground meat1 to 2 days
Fresh poultry1 to 2 days
Fresh fish, shrimp, scallops1 to 2 days
Raw roasts, steaks and chops3 to 5 days
Sliced bacon7 days
Lunch meat, opened3 to 5 days
Lunch meat, unopened2 weeks
Cooked meat dishes and leftovers3 to 4 days

If raw ground meat or chicken will not be cooked in a day or two, Illinois Extension says to freeze it instead. Moving it from the bottom shelf to the freezer is often the single best save of the week.

Leftover canned food

Once a can is open, its food is perishable. Clemson Cooperative Extension says to move it into a glass or plastic container, cover it tightly and refrigerate it. Their times for opened canned food:

  • Meat, seafood and poultry: 2 days
  • Vegetables: 3 days
  • Tomato sauce and fruit: 5 to 7 days
  • Pickles and olives: 1 to 2 months

Make an "eat first" spot

The FDA suggests creating a designated space in your fridge for foods that will go bad within a few days. A small bin or one end of a shelf works well. Put the half bag of spinach, the last two tortillas and the open container of sour cream there.

When you are deciding what to cook, look in that bin first. It turns "what do we have?" into a short, obvious list.

First in, first out

When you unpack groceries, put the new items behind the older ones. Utah State University Extension calls this "first in, first out." It means the older yogurt is in front and gets eaten before the new one. It takes a few extra seconds after shopping and stops the classic problem of finding a forgotten jar at the back months later.

Don't overpack

A packed fridge feels efficient, but it works against you. The FDA says not to crowd the refrigerator or freezer so tightly that air cannot circulate, because cold air has to move around food to keep it chilled. A crowded fridge also hides food, which is how things get forgotten.

If your fridge is always full, try buying a little less fresh food per trip. The FDA also suggests checking your fridge before you shop so you know what you already have.

Keep it clean

The FDA recommends cleaning the fridge regularly and wiping spills right away. A good rhythm:

  • Right away: wipe up any spill, especially meat juice.
  • Every week: check the thermometer, clear out anything spoiled and wipe the shelves you emptied. The night before trash day is an easy reminder.
  • Every few months: take everything out, wash the shelves and drawers, and put it all back in the right zones.

Small habits that help

  • Use clear containers so leftovers are visible.
  • Write the date on leftovers with a marker or masking tape.
  • Put cut or peeled vegetables in the fridge. The FDA notes that refrigerating them keeps them fresh.
  • Get leftovers into the fridge or freezer within two hours. The FDA says one hour if it is above 90°F outside.
  • Keep the door closed as much as you can. Standing with it open lets cold air out.

Common mistakes (and easy fixes)

  • Trusting the dial. A setting of "4" is not a temperature. Only a thermometer tells you if the fridge is at 40°F or below.
  • Milk on the door. The door is the warmest spot. The EPA says not to store milk or eggs there, and the FDA says the same for eggs.
  • Raw meat up high. A package of chicken on the top shelf can drip onto everything below it. Keep it low, in a container.
  • Raw meat beside raw vegetables. Illinois Extension says never to store raw vegetables on the same shelf as raw meat.
  • Bread in the fridge. North Dakota State University Extension says cold slows mold but makes bread go stale six times faster than at room temperature. To keep bread longer, freeze it.
  • Butter left open. Ohio State University Extension notes that butter can pick up fridge odors and go rancid after about a month. Wrap it or keep it covered.
  • Food left in an open can. Move it to a covered glass or plastic container, as above.
  • Washing berries the day you buy them. Wait until you eat them.
  • A fridge packed to the walls. Cold air needs room to move, and you need room to see.

A 10-minute weekly fridge reset

Once a week, the day before you shop, works well. Here is one way to do it in about ten minutes:

  1. Minute 1: read the thermometer. If it is above 40°F, turn the setting colder.
  2. Minutes 2 to 4: pull out leftovers and anything opened. Toss leftovers older than 3 to 4 days and anything with mold or an off smell.
  3. Minutes 5 to 6: check the bottom shelf. Raw meat you won't cook in the next day or two goes to the freezer.
  4. Minute 7: wipe the shelves you just emptied.
  5. Minute 8: fill the "eat first" bin with what needs eating this week.
  6. Minutes 9 to 10: write down what is missing for your shopping list, and what is already there so you don't buy it twice.

If the power goes out

An organized fridge also makes a power cut easier to handle, because you know what is in it. The FDA's advice:

  • Keep the fridge and freezer doors closed as much as you can.
  • An unopened fridge keeps food cold for about four hours.
  • A full freezer holds its temperature for about 48 hours if the door stays closed.
  • If the freezer thermometer reads 40°F or below, the food is safe and may be refrozen.
  • Throw out perishable food that has been above 40°F in the fridge for four hours or more. The FDA warns that you can't rely on appearance or odor to judge it.

Here a thermometer pays for itself. Without one, you are guessing.

Quick answers

What temperature should my fridge be?

At or below 40°F (4°C), per the FDA. The freezer should be at 0°F (-18°C).

Where is the coldest part of the fridge?

The EPA says the lower shelves are the coldest, and the door is the warmest. That is why raw meat goes low and condiments go on the door.

Can eggs go on the door?

No. The FDA says to keep eggs in their carton on a shelf inside the fridge. Virginia Cooperative Extension adds that the carton also cuts down on eggs soaking up odors and flavors.

Should bread go in the fridge?

Usually not. It goes stale faster there. Freeze what you won't eat in a few days, and throw out bread that grows mold.

How long can raw chicken stay in the fridge?

One to two days, per Illinois Extension. After that, cook it or freeze it.

Is freezer burn dangerous?

No. The FDA calls freezer burn a quality issue, not a safety issue. The food may be dry or tough in spots, but it is not unsafe because of the burn.

How often should I clean the fridge?

Wipe spills right away and clean regularly. A quick wipe each week and a full clean every few months is an easy rhythm. Illinois Extension notes that regular cleaning also cuts down on food odors.
